Under the pale glow of the midnight moon, the courtyard of the Zaha Inn becomes the stage for a psychological masterclass in underworld discipline. Sitting casually at the outdoor wooden bench, Cho Gwan, the arrogant second brother of the local crime family, looks down his nose at our boy Yi Zaha. Puffed up with unearned pride, the enforcer brags that their gang just received official backing from the regional Black Cat Union, meaning their criminal syndicate now holds absolute jurisdiction over the entire prefecture. In Cho Gwan's twisted mind, this scrawny twenty-year-old errand boy is nothing more than an insolent bug who ruined business at the brothel and needs to be squashed to set an example. With theatrical cruelty, Cho Gwan demands that Zaha give him three good reasons why he shouldn't be executed on the spot before his men finish digging the grave. But looking closely at Zaha’s face, you see zero fear, zero panic, and an almost clinical level of disappointment. When Cho Gwan reaches forward to grab our boy's collar, Zaha doesn’t bother with grand martial speeches. In an imperceptible blur of speed, he draws the cold steel dagger acquired from the blacksmith and drives it straight through Cho Gwan’s outstretched hand, pinning his palm to the wooden tabletop with a sickening, splintering thud. Before the screaming gangster can even process the pain, Zaha delivers a flurry of precision slashes that systematically sever his tendons, dropping the burly enforcer to his knees in the dirt. This is where we see the profound moral distinction that separates the Crazy Demon from ordinary martial sociopaths. While the world labels Zaha an unhinged lunatic, our boy actually operates under a remarkably clear, unforgiving moral compass. He doesn't strike out of petty vengeance; he interrogates Cho Gwan about his past crimes, confirming that this man was directly responsible for kidnapping vulnerable villagers, running human trafficking rings, and murdering innocent townspeople who couldn't pay extortion fees. In Zaha’s philosophy, scumbags who prey on the weak forfeit their right to breathe, and with a swift, clean thrust to the throat, he permanently terminates Cho Gwan's criminal career right beside the hole he ordered dug. The three masked shovel workers freeze in petrified silence, dropping their tools as they realize the quiet waiter standing over their boss's corpse is an apex predator. But instead of slaughtering the terrified laborers, Zaha orders them to remove their masks, immediately recognizing that these young men aren't hardened killers, but desperate country boys swept into gang life just to avoid starvation. This moment reveals the true genius of our boy’s chaotic pragmatism. Most regressed martial masters obsess over selfish revenge, but Zaha immediately focuses on stabilizing everyday life and protecting common folk. When one of the young men timidly admits he has nowhere to go and asks if the inn's kitchen has an opening, Zaha doesn't laugh; he conducts a deadpan midnight job interview right over a fresh grave, asking if the kid knows how to make authentic chicken noodle soup. When the boy enthusiastically swears he can make a delicious broth from scratch, Zaha hires him on the spot. Watching the new recruit carefully bury Cho Gwan's body and pack down the soil with military precision, Zaha realizes that to survive the upcoming storm, he cannot just fight every battle alone with bare knuckles. He needs a functional intelligence network, disciplined subordinates, and a legitimate economic foundation to outmaneuver the Black Cat Union before their leadership catches wind of Cho Gwan's sudden disappearance. In a single night, our boy liquidated a sadistic mob enforcer, saved three misguided youths from the underworld, and solved the inn’s kitchen staffing shortage. But with an entire regional syndicate looming in the shadows, this midnight skirmish is merely the opening spark of an inevitable underworld war.
