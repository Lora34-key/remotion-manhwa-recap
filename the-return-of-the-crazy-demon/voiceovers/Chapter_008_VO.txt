Watching his younger brother's executioner casually reclining on his personal master seat, Cho Il-gwon completely loses his composure, his face twisting in a violent mixture of disbelief and blind rage. He cannot accept the humiliating reality that an insignificant errand boy who cleans spilled tea for copper coins walked into his compound and butchered his brother. Lunging forward with a bloodthirsty roar, the syndicate leader channels decades of accumulated inner strength into a devastating palm strike designed to cave in our boy's chest. But Yi Zaha doesn't even bother rising from the wooden throne. With the cold, effortless detachment of a master swatting away an annoying fly, Zaha casually raises an open palm and meets the incoming strike dead-on. An explosive shockwave detonates through the council hall, shattering floorboards and sending Cho Il-gwon flying backward across the carpet, gasping in horror as his fingers tremble with agonizing numbness. This single clash shatters the mob boss’s entire worldview: by every law of martial progression, a twenty-year-old commoner shouldn't possess internal energy denser than a seasoned warlord. But Zaha isn't just an awakened novice; he carries the compressed primordial essence of the Heavenly Pearl and the combat refinement of a thousand life-and-death wars. Before the terrified boss can draw his steel, Zaha closes the distance in a ghost-like flash, seizing Cho Il-gwon by the throat and slamming his spine against the reinforced pillar. What follows is a cold, calculated indictment of the syndicate's pathetic existence. Zaha looks into the choking man’s eyes and calls him fundamentally sloppy, pointing out that true strength creates order and protects territory, whereas the Cho family spent years preying on impoverished street vendors and selling out their own town to foreign syndicates just to line their pockets. In our boy’s eyes, a ruler who parasitizes his own people is worse than vermin, and with a brutal downward slam that pulverizes the stone floor, he permanently closes the Cho family's reign of terror. When the estate guards finally rush into the hall with drawn sabers, Zaha simply scoffs at their uselessness, systematically clearing out the compound, seizing the gang's hidden slush funds, and disappearing into the night before the authorities even realize a coup occurred. Returning to his humble room at the inn, Zaha sits cross-legged to consolidate his breakthrough, guiding the silver qi through his twelve major meridians to complete a legendary martial milestone known as marrow cleansing. From a cultivation standpoint, this is a monumental moment: in his previous life, Zaha's meridians were permanently warped by flawed techniques and chronic inflammation, which was the literal root cause of his psychotic episodes and descent into madness. But by purging those blockages right now in his youth, he is actively laying an indestructible, heavenly foundation that will allow him to reach realms far beyond mortal limits. Most epic cultivation novels describe marrow cleansing as a majestic, glowing spiritual awakening, but the physical reality is hilariously grounded in biology: expelling twenty years of toxic cellular impurities through your skin produces an odor so horrifying it could qualify as a war crime. When Cha Sung-tae arrives the next morning to deliver his daily intelligence report, he kicks open the inn door, only to violently gag on his own breath as tears stream down his face. Covering his silk sleeves over his nose, the horrified manager frantically screams, asking if the new grandmaster of the underworld somehow had an uncontrollable digestive accident inside his bedroom. Staring back through the rancid haze with dead-eyed judgment, Zaha questions whether his right-hand man truly values his life, proving that even after single-handedly overthrowing a criminal empire, dealing with an insolent subordinate remains our boy's most exhausting daily challenge.
