Inside the shadowy, incense-clouded war room of the Black Rabbit Union headquarters, a scene of pure corporate underworld humiliation is playing out. Sitting atop a carved wooden throne, wearing his signature black rabbit mask with hollow eyeholes, the supreme Union Leader summons his intelligence executives to get an urgent operational status report on the Golden Phoenix Pavilion. What he expects to hear is that Pavilion Master Ban Sa-woong successfully crushed the insubordination in Ilyang Prefecture and collected the quarterly extortion gold. Instead, Manager Byuk nervously clears his throat and drops a bomb that shatters the entire room's composure: Sa-woong is dead, his mountain hunting lodge was thoroughly raided, and forensic traces show the elite pavilion master was folded in less than two minutes without mounting any meaningful resistance. Worse yet, not a single subordinate was killed; every guard, dog handler, and accountant abandoned their post and willingly defected to the enemy, happily hauling away the syndicate's secret gold reserves. When the furious Union Leader demands to know what kind of legendary hidden master from a rival province orchestrated this coup, the executives tremble in sheer embarrassment, bowing their heads to the floor before mumbling that the culprit is a local kid named Lee Zaha, whose official occupational title is tavern errand boy. The Union Leader lifts his rabbit mask in absolute, vein-popping frustration, having a complete existential crisis over the fact that a billion-won criminal syndicate just got thoroughly embarrassed by a teenager who sweeps noodles off tavern floors. Meanwhile, back in Ilyang Prefecture, Zaha is completely unconcerned with the impending syndicate retaliation, focusing his attention on something far more practical: turning Cha Sung-tae into a competent fighter so he stops playing dead every time an enemy draws a weapon. Zaha puts Sung-tae through an unhinged, military-style martial boot camp, making him run laps carrying heavy stone weights and drilling fundamental saber mechanics until Sung-tae is coughing up dust and questioning all of his life choices. What makes this training sequence so brilliant from a psychological perspective is how Zaha systematically breaks Sung-tae's cowardly mindset. Sung-tae is used to being a sleazy, low-tier extortionist who relies on bluffs and dirty tricks, but Zaha forces him to understand that true martial confidence comes from physical conditioning, spatial awareness, and absolute ruthlessness when the moment strikes. But Zaha is not just training his muscles; he is also introducing his secret weapon: digestive warfare. Remembering a bizarre herbal formula from his previous life involving rotten roots and intense laxative herbs, Zaha begins conceptualizing the Heavenly Golden Pill, an unholy concoction designed not to boost internal qi, but to completely paralyze an enemy's gastrointestinal tract in the middle of combat. Zaha reasons that no matter how supreme an enemy's sword technique is, nobody can maintain a refined combat stance while violently fighting for control of their bowels. As Sung-tae listens to his master describe biological warfare disguised as sacred Taoist medicine, he realizes with chilling clarity that Zaha is not just a martial genius; he is an absolute, certifiable maniac whose tactical methods defy every convention of honorable combat. It highlights the unpredictable brilliance of Zaha's leadership: he trains his allies with merciless discipline while preparing the most absurd, devastating psychological traps for the enemies marching toward his gates, proving that madness and strategy are two sides of the same coin. Under his eccentric guidance, a cowardly extortionist is slowly being forged into a disciplined lieutenant ready for the storm ahead. In this unforgiving world, survival belongs only to those who can fight with both blade and brain, and Zaha is more than ready to teach that lesson to anyone foolish enough to test him.
